## Lesson objective

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to turn a client consultation into a clear haircut plan: read the head and hair in front of you, explain what you’re going to do, choose products with intention, and set the client up for the right maintenance after they leave.

## The consultation changes here

In Chapter 1, you learned how to run a consultation that makes the client feel heard. You asked better questions. You listened before you responded. You learned how to close the gap between what a client asks for and what they actually expect.

Now we’re adding the technical layer.

Because a good consultation is not just a conversation. At some point, you have to turn what the client tells you into a plan you can execute with your hands.

That means looking at more than the reference photo. More than the haircut name. More than “short on the sides, longer on top.”

You need to read what’s actually in the chair.

## Read the head before you choose the cut

Before you start, take in the whole picture.

Head shape. Growth patterns. Crown behavior. Density. Texture. Current length. Condition. Face shape. The silhouette the client wants.

Those details are not separate from the haircut. They determine how the haircut is going to behave.

Two clients can show you the same reference and need completely different approaches. One might have the density to hold the shape. Another might need more weight left in a specific area. One crown might cooperate. Another might fight you the entire way if you ignore it.

The goal is not to force every client into the same technical formula. The goal is to understand what you’re working with before you make your first decision.

## Build the plan out loud

Once you understand what the client wants and what their hair will support, say the plan back to them.

Keep it simple. You do not need to give them a barber-school lecture. But they should understand the major decisions before you start.

How short are you taking the sides? Where is the taper or fade going to sit? How much length or weight are you keeping? What are you doing with the top? What kind of finish are you aiming for?

If there’s something you need to adjust from the reference, say it now.

That is the Artisan standard: no surprises at the mirror.

## The full service journey

The haircut is the center of the appointment, but the service starts before the cut and continues after it.

A complete service includes:

- Greeting and seating the client.
- Preparing the cape, station, and tools.
- Evaluating head shape and growth patterns.
- Assessing density, texture, length, and condition.
- Understanding the desired silhouette and confirming the haircut plan.
- Explaining realistic maintenance before and after the service.
- Finishing with intention, presenting the result in the mirror, and closing out the appointment professionally.

None of those steps are filler. Together, they are what makes the service feel considered instead of mechanical.

## Product is part of the haircut

Do not wait until the end of the service and grab whatever product happens to be closest.

Before you recommend anything, answer five questions:

- Does the client want a matte, natural, low-shine, or polished finish?
- Do they need light, flexible, medium, or strong hold?
- Should the product go into damp hair or dry hair?
- Are you trying to create control, texture, volume, movement, or moisture?
- How much time is this client realistically going to spend styling at home?

The right product is the one that supports the haircut and the person wearing it.

If the client cannot reproduce the finish at home, the service is not fully finished. Show them how much product to use, where to apply it, and what they should expect it to do.

## Start building your book now

Technique gets better through repetition. A book gets built the same way.

For this chapter, you are not waiting for practice clients to magically appear. You are going to build a pool of people you can work with intentionally.

Start with 25 names. Friends. Family. Neighbors. Former coworkers. People from the gym. Community connections. Referrals. Anyone you can approach professionally and who understands that you are working under supervision.

From that list, send 10 invitations and book your first three supervised models.

The goal here is bigger than getting three haircuts on the calendar. You are learning how to create your own repetition instead of waiting for opportunity to come to you.
